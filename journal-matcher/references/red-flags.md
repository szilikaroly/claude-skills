# The integrity screen

## The stance

This screen is **positive-signal based**: it asks whether a journal shows the marks
of a real one, and reports where those marks are missing. It is deliberately not a
blacklist.

Blacklists age badly, are contested, and are unfair to new legitimate journals —
particularly journals from outside North America and Western Europe, which are the
ones a naive filter punishes hardest. A missing signal is a reason to look, not a
verdict. Report flags as *"here is what to check before you commit"*, never as
*"this journal is fraudulent"*.

The person submitting makes the call. Give them what they need to make it.

## The automated checks

| Flag | What it means |
|---|---|
| not in the NLM Catalog | No PubMed record at all. For clinical work this is usually disqualifying on its own. |
| in PubMed but not MEDLINE-indexed | Often a PMC-only deposit. Users conflate these constantly; CV assessment does not. Check whether MEDLINE indexing was ever held and lost — de-listing is a serious signal. |
| not in the Scimago/Scopus file | Either not Scopus-indexed, or too new to have an SJR. Distinguish these: check Elsevier's source list directly. |
| OA but not in DOAJ | DOAJ listing is cheap and standard for a legitimate OA journal. Absence is worth a question, and is not conclusive — DOAJ applications take months. |
| no registered ISSN | Nearly always disqualifying. Registration is free and trivial. |
| declares ≤3 weeks to publication | Self-declared in DOAJ. Real peer review of a clinical paper does not finish in three weeks. |
| median ≤14 days submission→acceptance | *Measured* from PubMed history dates across ≥10 papers. Much stronger than the self-declared version, and much harder to explain away. |
| DOAJ says no plagiarism screening | A basic control that legitimate journals run. |
| charges an APC while neither MEDLINE-indexed nor in DOAJ | Money in, no quality infrastructure visible. The core predatory pattern. |
| output grew more than 8× in three years | The special-issue farm signature: guest-edited issues multiplying faster than any editorial board could review. This is what precedes de-listing. |
| ≥60% of authors from one country, across few countries | A narrow authorship base. Can be entirely legitimate (a national society journal); can also mean the editorial board and the author pool are the same small group. |

## What the screen cannot see — check these by hand

- **Editorial board reality.** Are members named with affiliations? Do they exist,
  and do they know they are listed? Board members being unaware they are on a
  masthead is a recurring finding in predatory-journal investigations.
- **The invitation you received.** A flattering mass email about your "esteemed
  contribution", with a deadline weeks away for a special issue on a theme only
  loosely related to your work, is the recruitment mechanism. Legitimate guest-editor
  invitations are specific about why *you* and about the issue's theme.
- **Publisher membership.** COPE, OASPA, DOAJ, STM — checkable in minutes on those
  bodies' own member lists, not on the publisher's claim page.
- **Whether the journal was recently de-listed** from Scopus, MEDLINE or Web of
  Science. This is the single strongest signal available and it is not in any of the
  files this skill reads. Search for it.
- **Where the DOIs resolve, and who owns the domain.** Hijacked journals — a clone
  site impersonating a real indexed journal — pass most metric checks because the
  metrics belong to the real journal. Verify the URL against the ISSN's registered
  homepage.

## Reporting

Say what the flag is, what it would mean if confirmed, and what to check. Then stop.

> *Regional Cardiology Notes* charges ~$1,490 and is neither MEDLINE-indexed nor in
> DOAJ, and its output went from 120 to 4,100 papers a year since 2023. Both patterns
> are associated with special-issue farming and with subsequent Scopus de-listing.
> Before submitting: check whether it was recently de-listed, and whether its
> editorial board members are real and aware they are listed.

Never write "this is a predatory journal". Write what was found.

For a genuinely borderline case, send the user to **Think.Check.Submit**
(thinkchecksubmit.org), the **DOAJ** entry, the **COPE member list**, and — where
their institution has access — the **Scopus source list** and **JCR** directly.
